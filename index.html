⛈️ Storms and sea level

Storms raise the water (surge) and send big waves for a few days. The sim slows down so you can watch.

Storm peaks at

Maine's tides rise and fall about 2.6 m twice a day. The same storm can do much more damage when it peaks at high tide.

Portland, Maine's tide gauge has recorded roughly 2 mm/yr since 1912. Try 10–15 mm/yr for late-century projections.

Beach faces is the compass direction straight out to sea. Wave turning is how much headlands and the bay bend the offshore waves clockwise before they arrive. 🧮 The math behind your beach

The model tracks the shoreline position \(y\) at every spot \(x\) along the beach as time \(t\) passes. Some parts of the equation are always working; others switch on when you build something, add sand or change the weather. New parts appear in colour.

      🔬 How the model works

      This is a one-line model, the workhorse of shoreline engineering since Pelnard-Considère (1956). It treats the whole beach profile as one line that moves back and forth without changing shape, so the only thing that matters is how much sand flows along the shore.

      Waves that arrive at an angle drive a current in the surf zone that carries sand along the beach. Where that flow speeds up, the beach loses sand and retreats; where it slows down (behind a groin, in the calm lee of a breakwater) the beach builds out. A second, faster part of the model moves sand between the beach and an offshore bar as waves grow and shrink, so the beach narrows in storms and heals in calm weather. Sea-level rise shifts the whole profile landward (the Bruun rule).

      Tides, dunes and overwash. During a storm the sim resolves the tide (Maine's semidiurnal tide, 12.42 hours). Waves run up the beach (Stockdon et al., 2006); when sea level + tide + surge + run-up climbs above the dune toe, the dune is cut back (Larson et al., 2004, with coefficients fit to Camp Ellis). A wide beach lifts the toe, so it shields the dune. Once the dune is gone, waves wash over onto the road and flood houses. Between storms, wind slowly rebuilds dunes on wide beaches.

      Waves blocked and focused. Headlands and long jetties shape the waves that reach the beach. A rocky headland blocks waves arriving from its side, leaving a calm shadow in its lee, and no sand gets around it. Waves that hit a headland or a long jetty bounce back and pile up on top of the incoming waves, so the beach just updrift sees waves up to 25% bigger (as beside the Camp Ellis jetty).

      Dune grass. Planted beach grass binds the dune with its roots and traps blowing sand. On planted stretches the model makes storm erosion 40% slower and post-storm recovery 30% faster (illustrative values). Grass does not change longshore drift, and it stops working where the sea reaches the dune.

      Shorebirds. Piping plovers and least terns, both endangered in Maine, nest on open, dry sand in front of the dunes from May through August. A nesting area stays safe while there is at least 20 m of dry beach in front of the dune, is at risk below that, and is lost below 8 m (for example in front of a seawall). A storm during nesting season washes out nests on narrow beaches. At the end of each season, safe areas fledge about 1.44 chicks per pair, Maine's statewide rate in 2025.

      The sim solves these equations with finite differences on 150 cells, each 10 m long, taking hundreds of small time steps per second.

      🤔 What it leaves out

      • Waves are the same everywhere along the beach except near structures and headlands; real bays like Saco Bay focus and shelter waves unevenly.
      • Wave conditions are monthly averages rather than real, hour-by-hour sea states, so drift rates are on the high side.
      • Dunes and overwash are simplified to one number per 10 m of beach; tidal inlets, rip currents and wave reflection off seawalls are not modelled.
      • The season climate is illustrative: winter swell from the east–northeast, calm summers with south–southeast swell, shaped by the UNE Camp Ellis buoy record and NOAA buoy 44007.

      Use it to build intuition, not to forecast a real beach.
