Storms raise the water (surge) and send big waves for a few days. The sim slows down so you can watch.

Storm peaks at

Maine's tides rise and fall about 2.6 m twice a day. The same storm can do much more damage when it peaks at high tide.

Portland, Maine's tide gauge has recorded roughly 2 mm/yr since 1912. Try 10–15 mm/yr for late-century projections.

🧮 The math behind your beach

The model tracks the shoreline \(y_1\) and an offshore contour \(y_2\) at every spot \(x\) along the beach as time \(t\) passes. Some parts of the equation are always working; others switch on when you build something, add sand or change the weather. New parts appear in colour.

      🔬 How the model works

      This is a two-line model (Bakker, 1968), a step up from the classic one-line model of Pelnard-Considère (1956). It follows two lines along the beach: the shoreline, and an offshore depth contour about 2.5 m deep (the dotted line in the sea). Each line moves back and forth as sand flows along the shore, and sand also moves between them: storms pull it off the beach onto an offshore bar, and calm waves bring it back. Short groins block only the inner line; long ones block both.

      Waves that arrive at an angle drive a current in the surf zone that carries sand along the beach. Where that flow speeds up, the beach loses sand and retreats; where it slows down (behind a groin, in the calm lee of a breakwater) the beach builds out. Sea-level rise shifts the whole profile landward (the Bruun rule).

      Refraction and diffraction. The sim computes the wave crests over the whole sea bed by solving the eikonal equation for the wave phase, with the water depth from the two lines and the wave speed from linear wave theory. Crests slow down and bend to face the shore in shallow water (refraction), and wrap around the ends of structures into their shadows (diffraction). The wave angle where they break drives the sand, so salients behind breakwaters and the calm lee of groins come from the waves themselves.

      Tides, dunes and overwash. During a storm the sim resolves the tide (Maine's semidiurnal tide, 12.42 hours). Waves run up the beach (Stockdon et al., 2006); when sea level + tide + surge + run-up climbs above the dune toe, the dune is cut back (Larson et al., 2004, with coefficients fit to Camp Ellis). A wide beach lifts the toe, so it shields the dune. Once the dune is gone, waves wash over onto the road and flood houses. Between storms, wind slowly rebuilds dunes on wide beaches.

      Waves blocked and focused. Headlands and long jetties shape the waves that reach the beach. A rocky headland blocks waves arriving from its side, leaving a calm shadow in its lee, and no sand gets around it. Waves that hit a headland or a long jetty bounce back and pile up on top of the incoming waves, so the beach just updrift sees waves up to 25% bigger (as beside the Camp Ellis jetty).

      Dune grass. Planted beach grass binds the dune with its roots and traps blowing sand. On planted stretches the model makes storm erosion 40% slower and post-storm recovery 30% faster (illustrative values). Grass does not change longshore drift, and it stops working where the sea reaches the dune.

      Shorebirds. Piping plovers and least terns, both endangered in Maine, nest on open, dry sand in front of the dunes from May through August. A nesting area stays safe while there is at least 20 m of dry beach in front of the dune, is at risk below that, and is lost below 8 m (for example in front of a seawall). A storm during nesting season washes out nests on narrow beaches. At the end of each season, safe areas fledge about 1.44 chicks per pair, Maine's statewide rate in 2025 (Camp Ellis and Pine Point use their own, much lower 2025 rate of about 0.23, and Wells its record 2). A colony needs roughly 1.25 chicks per pair just to hold steady, so colonies above that slowly grow and those below it shrink. Plovers nest in open sand and sparse young grass; only thick, established grass that has built out onto the beach pushes them toward the water.

      The sim solves the shoreline equations with finite differences on 150 cells, each 10 m long, taking hundreds of small time steps per second, and recomputes the wave field on a 5 m grid (about 42,000 points) four times a second with a fast-sweeping solver.

      🤔 What it leaves out
      • Line models assume gently curving shorelines. Where a shoreline turns sharply (the sides of a tombolo, say) the sim caps the wave angle used for sand transport at 45° and lets over-steep stretches slump sideways, so tombolos and salients come out smooth and a little broader than a detailed 2D model would give.
      • The wave field uses geometric (ray) optics: refraction and the bending of crests around obstacles are included, but wave heights in shadows use a simpler sheltering factor, and the sea bed is idealized from two contour lines.
      • Wave conditions are monthly averages rather than real, hour-by-hour sea states, so drift rates are on the high side.
      • Dunes and overwash are simplified to one number per 10 m of beach; tidal inlets, rip currents and wave reflection off seawalls are not modelled.
      • The season climate is illustrative: winter swell from the east–northeast, calm summers with south–southeast swell, shaped by the UNE Camp Ellis buoy record and NOAA buoy 44007.

      Use it to build intuition, not to forecast a real beach.
